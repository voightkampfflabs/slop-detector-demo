The shop opens at nine but I am usually there by eight, because the first hour is the only one in which nobody asks me anything. I put the kettle on, pull the shutters halfway and look at whatever was left on the stand the evening before. This morning it was a steel touring frame from the late eighties, brought in by a man who said he had found it in his father's garage and wanted to ride it to the coast in June. The paint was a dull green that had gone chalky along the top tube, and the rear wheel would not turn at all.

People assume the hard part of an old bicycle is the rust. It is not. Rust on a frame like this is mostly on the surface, and a rag with a little oil takes it back to something you can live with. The hard part is everything that was greased once, in a factory, and never again. Grease does not disappear. It dries into a brown wax that glues the parts together, so the seat post will not come out of the frame, the pedals will not come off the cranks and the bearings feel as if somebody had packed them with sand.

I started with the rear hub because a wheel that does not turn tells you the most. The axle nuts came off with a long spanner and some patience. Inside, the cones were pitted on one side, a ring of small grey craters where the balls had sat in the same place for thirty years. You can see from that which way the bike leaned in the garage. New cones for this hub have not been made for a long time, so I went through the drawer of parts I keep from wheels nobody collected, and found a pair that was close enough. They are a little wider than the originals, which means the spacing has to be made up on the other side with a thinner washer.

The owner came back at lunch to see how it was going. He wanted to know whether it was worth it, which is the question everybody asks and the one I am worst at answering. A new bike that does the same job costs less than the hours I will put into this one. On the other hand the frame is straight, the tubes are good, and his father chose it, measured himself for it and rode it to work for eleven years. I told him the truth, that it depends on what he is paying for. He thought about it and said to carry on.

By four the wheel turned freely, and I had the seat post soaking in penetrating oil with a note taped to the saddle so that nobody would try to force it. That will take two days, perhaps three. You turn it a few degrees each morning and each evening and one day it simply lets go. The cables, the chain and the brake blocks I will replace without thinking about it, because rubber and thin steel do not get a second life. The saddle is leather and has shrunk into a hard brown shape that fits nobody, but he asked me to keep it, so I rubbed some fat into it and hung it near the radiator, not too close.

The last thing I did before closing was to write the list on the card that hangs from the handlebars. Hub cones, replaced. Bottom bracket, to be opened. Headset, loose, probably only needs adjusting. Front rim, a flat spot near the valve that I can live with if he can. It is not a long list for a bicycle of that age, and most of it is cleaning. I turned off the lamp over the stand, pulled the shutters down and left the green frame hanging there with its one good wheel, turning slowly until it stopped.
